The situation

FDOT needed commercial roofing work at several sites inside one program, one budget and one schedule. Each site sat in a different town, so each had its own permit office, inspection process and access rules.

The real challenge was running all of it in parallel: permits, materials, crews and equipment moving between locations, without one site's delay pulling down the others.

What I owned

I owned the program from planning through closeout: the phased schedule, the budget, permitting at every location, procurement, mobilization and tooling, subcontractor coordination and progress tracking. I was the single point of contact between our crew, the subcontractors, architects, general contractors and inspectors.

The decision: sequence by readiness

I sequenced the program by site readiness rather than starting every location at once. A site moved into production only when its permit was approved, its materials were scheduled for delivery and its crew and equipment were assigned. Sites still waiting on approvals stayed in preparation instead of tying up crews.

How I ran it

  1. Program planning. I built a phased Waterfall plan with phase-gate reviews, a baseline budget and one standard scope of work applied to every site.
  2. Permitting by jurisdiction. I tracked each town's permit requirements, submittals and inspection requests separately, so every location had its own approval path.
  3. Procurement and material staging. I issued purchase orders, tracked supplier lead times and timed deliveries to each site's start date, with staging planned per location.
  4. Mobilization and tooling. I planned each move to site, including crew assignments and the equipment and tooling the roof at that location required.
  5. Subcontractor management. I set the scope split between our crew and the subcontractors, reviewed their scopes of work and kept everyone working to one schedule.
  6. Budget and schedule control. I tracked cost against the budget and progress against the schedule in advanced Excel, and routed every scope change through structured change approval before work changed.

Risk management

  • Weather. Roofing in Florida means planning around rain, so work was scheduled to keep open roof areas protected and watertight at the end of each day.
  • Safety. Every site followed OSHA requirements, including fall protection, along with its local building codes.
  • Inspections. Required inspections were built into the schedule as hold points, so work did not move past a stage before it was signed off.
  • Tracking. I kept a risk mitigation plan and an issue log for the whole program, covering every location.

Closeout

Each site closed out with final inspections, a punch list of remaining items and handover of the completed work to the client.

The result

  • A $2M to $5M FDOT roofing program managed across multiple locations over about 6 to 8 months.
  • Permits, materials, crews and subcontractors coordinated for every site under one plan and one budget.
  • OSHA and local building code requirements followed at every location.
